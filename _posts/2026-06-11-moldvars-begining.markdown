## Preface
The original title of the book was most likely *Moldvars Uvedruns*, 'The Books of Moldvar'. Yet because the text breaks off unfinished, in later times it came to be known as *Moldvars Teiksna*, 'Of the Founding of Moldvar'.  

The author was a black-robed brother named Tis, of whom nothing is known save his name. Most likely he was neither master of the library nor one of its more important household brethren. Neither is it known to which princess or lady of Lingvar he dedicated his work. Perhaps it was but a literary device, though the mention of mice seems to suggest that there truly was an addressee.  

The text speaks of an earlier Book of Moldvar, written by the brother Narko, which has since been lost. Most likely it perished in the burning of Moldvar, caused by Berglar. Some scholars hold that Tis sought to restore that earlier book from the accounts of those who had once read it. Yet the work was interrupted for reasons unknown, and thus the manuscript breaks off midway. What befell the author remains a mystery to this day.  

## Introduction by Tis

To thee, my lady, and at thy bidding, though I am ashamed to say it, I offer these fragments.

When I spoke to thee of the mice, know that I did not jest. For they know the history of the library better than any, and they are loath to share it with simple brethren.

What was lacking I have supplied by questioning the eldest of the household. It is little enough, and my own skill is less than that of those who came before me. Therefore forgive me, I pray thee, and accept this small gift, unworthy though it be of thy person. 

## I. The Hermitage of Molds

It was in the third year of the reign of Insuves Izarn.

There was a man called Molds among the king’s company. He asked to be released from service, and it was granted unto him, for he had served the king faithfully for many years.

Gathering his possessions, he went into the Turola Mountains in search of a place wherein he might find rest. Passing the left tributary of the Nirsel, he came unto the foothills, which seemed unto him far enough removed from the dwellings of men and from all trade roads. There Molds built himself a hut beside a stream and dwelt therein.

Molds lived as a hermit. He ate no meat, but sustained himself only upon that which he grew with his own hands or found within the forest. And nearby stood Thomgravs[^1], a great wood of silmenis trees and onsbrots, thick and dark. The vilas came there from the mountains because of Ruganskaps[^2], which was there.

One day the vilas saw Molds gathering honey from a tree-hive, and they said unto him:

“Come unto us.”

And he obeyed them. He came and dwelt among them for a time.

Soon word spread abroad that there lived in Moldsrun a man who was friend to the vilas. Men began to visit him, and he told them all that he had learned.  

## II. The Founding of the Library of Moldvar
When King Izarn heard of this, he commanded that Molds be brought before him, that he might learn the wisdom known unto him. Yet Molds would not come.

“Come unto me and reveal the secrets of the vilas,” said Izarn. “For they share them but grudgingly with men, and I desire greatly to know them.”

Molds answered through messengers:

“Come thyself and learn.”

The king’s men were greatly angered at this. They demanded that Molds lose his head for such an insult. Yet Izarn would not consent.

“There is something more in this matter,” said he. “Even I must go, if I wish to hear his words.”

Yet there was war then with the Insuveses of Andomir[^3]. Izarn forgot Molds, and many things came to pass thereafter.

Only later did Agint[^4], daughter of Izarn, journey unto Moldsrun.

But Molds was already dead. In the place of his hut a mound had been raised above him where he had died. Nearby, the companions of Molds, who had grown numerous over the years, had built houses for themselves and lived as he had lived.

Having heard their tales, Agint gave over her dowry that a manor might be built there.

“It is not fitting,” she said, “that men who possess such fair knowledge should dwell in pits and hovels. But that all who wish may partake of it, let it be written down in books.”

At her urging a superior was chosen also, a master who should lead the fellowship. Hence arose the custom that the household proclaims the master, while the ruler confirms him. 

## III. Of Master Narko
After Molds, the first master of Moldvar was Narko, the eldest of his disciples. He was held to be a friend of the vilas and often dwelt in Thomgravs. From him comes the custom of the southern gathering in the forest.

Narko commanded that books be written containing the lore of the vilas. Among them, as I believe, was *Rugan Gerbutun*, which was later devoured. He was the first editor of *Moldvars Uvedrun*, wherein all the tales were set down.

Yet Narko was not a lover of books. Following the example of the vilas, he commanded his successors to learn the stories by heart, so that their minds might become books from which one could read when paper failed.

He died in the eighteenth year of his rule over Moldvar. They buried him where they had buried his predecessor. Today there stands a cloister garden there, which any man who wishes may visit. 

## IV. Master Goil
After the death of Narko, no new master was chosen for a long while, for it was commonly held that none was fit to take his place.

At length the princess of the Insuveses began urging the brethren to choose themselves a guide in their work. Yet one after another refused. So greatly was the former master loved.

Among them was Goil, the youngest of the household, still but a child. Because of his youth, no labour was given unto him. He tended only the clearing in Tōmgravs, that it should not be overgrown. Moreover, he sang beautifully and knew many songs in Areile.

Goil taught the others to sing. He urged also that every gathering should begin thus, whether upon the clearings, at work, or even at meals. His voice was clear and strong. Not so melodious as that of the vilas, yet no less fair.

Goil died after a brief illness. Five years did he lead Moldvar.

After his death, lightning struck a tree in Tōmgravs, and part of the forest was consumed by fire. In that same year Vesurrod was born[^5].

For a long time thereafter there was none to lead Moldvar.

## V. The Dispute Concerning Books
When they debated whom they should choose as the new master, and there was none willing to accept without hesitation, one among the companions, whose name was Hant, spoke thus:

“Since the vilas remember their songs, so ought we to do. Words must not be written in books.”

And they listened unto him.

After some time a messenger came from the king to collect the Chronicles of Lingvar.

“Give me the books,” said he.

“We have none,” they answered.

Then arose a dispute. At last the king summoned Hant unto himself. Hant came to Vosdena together with several companions.

“It was permitted unto you to dwell in Moldvar so that you might write down knowledge,” said the king. “Why do ye not do so? Have ye lacked bark for parchment or ink for writing?”

“No, my lord,” answered Hant. “But we deemed that, according to the custom of those who shared their wisdom with us, we ought to remember all things.”

At these words the king laughed.

“The memory of men is most frail,” said he. “Therefore all things must be written down. Yet if ye desire to remain faithful to your masters, I shall not forbid it. Human knowledge, however, write down for me.”

And so it was done.

Thereafter the king questioned them concerning this wisdom of the vilas of which so much had been spoken. When they told him of it, he said:

“Such knowledge is of no use. These are but tales with which nurses entertain children. Small wonder that few care to listen unto you.”

Wishing to prove him mistaken, Hant offered to rid him of the dragon that had lately laid waste to the coast of Dalmun. The ruler agreed and even gave him a boat and provisions for the journey.

Thus Hant took his companions with him and, with the first ebbing tide, sailed eastward through the strait[^6].

## VI. The Tale of Vesurrod
Vesurrod was of the Etvings, son of Dernen and of Gubenita Komatötis.

His father possessed great estates in Dalmun, including a stronghold and a castle approached through the gate of Aiktarvrath.

Vesurrod had a brother of his own blood and a half-sister. She was called Rimtis. She was raised in the household of her uncle, and thus the siblings first met at Andsar[^7]. Vesurrod at first believed Rimtis to be but his cousin.

When the truth came to light, Dernen drove his son into exile. Rimtis he had already sent overseas to conceal his shame. There she was wed to one of the Angolts, and the son born of her and Vesurrod would later contribute to the downfall of the house.

Thus Vesurrod dwelt upon the coast among the rocks, where he found a band of exiles much like himself. Yet whereas they sought only wealth, he desired vengeance upon the father who had dealt with him so harshly.

“If he will not grant me the portion that is mine by right,” said Vesurrod unto his companions, “then I shall take for myself what belongs to me.”

And he spoke of Aiktarvrath, which was a rich stronghold.

The city was famed for its gate facing northward and guarded by two mighty towers. In each tower watchmen stood day and night, keeping watch not only upon those entering and leaving the city, but also upon the ships coming into the harbour.

Then Vesurrod went unto Aiktarvrath. When he was recognised, he said:

“I come to place myself into the hands of my father Dernen. Tell this to the castellan, that he may come unto me, for it is not fitting that a son of the Etvings should enter the stronghold like a beggar.”

The castellan was told of this and rejoiced. He came forth to greet Vesurrod.

Yet when he stepped upon the bridge where Vesurrod waited, Vesurrod struck him and cast him into the sea, so that he perished.

Then they seized Vesurrod and cast him into a dungeon to await the coming of his father. Yet he cared little for this, and even sent word to the rest of his band that they should enter the city in secret and await further command.

When Dernen arrived, he ordered that his son be brought before him. They led him in chains and set him before his father. Then Dernen commanded that he be unbound.

“Permit me, father,” said the son of Dernen, “to speak before thou dost judge me.”

Then he approached his father and threw his arms about him. Those gathered there thought that he sought mercy with tears.

But Vesurrod seized his father’s throat with his teeth and tore it open.

Blood flowed abundantly upon the floor, and Dernen fell dead.

They say that before Vesurrod drew his sword against those assembled, he first drank his fill of his father’s life.

At that very moment the rest of his band sprang from hiding, cutting and slaying without restraint. On that day they killed all whom they found and afterward barricaded themselves within the palace.

Vesurrod promised them that they would rule over the whole city.

Yet in the night, while all slept, he transformed into a dragon, and his wrath was beyond that of men. 

##VII. Hant and Vesurrod
Thus did Hant find him when, of his own will, he journeyed unto Aiktarvrath.

The stronghold was in part burned, and few folk remained therein. The great gate lay fallen, and the dragon rested amid the ruins, stirring from that place only when hunger drove him forth.

Having come ashore, Hant entered the city from the far side, where once there had stood a lesser gate. He drew near unto the monster’s lair without being seen.

The dragon slept.

Then Hant took up his harp and began to sing a song of the vilas, called in their tongue Alkalene Vedalva.

When he came unto the verses that spoke of Riār, Vesurrod awoke and interrupted him.

“Thou art mistaken,” said he, “to accuse me of desiring only destruction, as did he. I would have done none of these things had my father not taken Rimtis from me. It is unto him that thou shouldst sing sorrowful songs, not unto me.”

Hant answered him not, but continued singing.

Then he sang the tale of Sōline and the journey of the vilas across Lethbrost[^8].

At this song Vesurrod laughed, though this time he listened unto the end.

“Before I devour thee,” said he, “know this: my father gave not his life for me, and thou shalt not compare him to the Sun-Daughter. He himself was my enemy, for not only did he take from me that which I desired, but he sought to punish me when I would not submit unto his judgement.”

Yet Hant answered him not, but sang on.

At last he sang the Song of the Names of Trees[^9].

Then Vesurrod began to weep.

*There is no salvation for man.*<br>
*His fate is sealed.*<br>
*From the day of birth unto death,*<br>
*The path leads but one way.*<br>

*In Bedubnis shalt thou end thy life,*<br>
*Whether rich or without fortune.*<br>
*Cursed are all who have fallen.*<br>
*Lowest of all, the son of Dernen.*<br>

When he had spoken these words, he begged that a spear be brought and driven through his reptilian heart.

But Hant would not consent.

Instead he took a rope that had been tied unto a bell and fashioned from it a halter, with which he bound the monster’s jaws.

Then, to the wonder of the people, he led him across the square and out through the gates of the city.

Vesurrod followed meekly as a lamb, allowing himself to be led wherever Hant desired.

And Hant led him far beyond the dwellings of men, by roads seldom trodden, until at last they came again unto Moldvar.

## VIII. The End of the Tale of Vesurrod
When the king learned of these events, he sent men to slay the dragon.

Yet Hant would not allow them to enter the grounds of the library.

“Come again in five years,” said he unto them. “Or in twelve.”

So great was his renown that they obeyed him, and none dared lay hand upon Vesurrod.

Thus the dragon dwelt among the brethren for many years.

At length, as some maintain, he returned unto the shape of a man. Thereafter Vesurrod departed from Moldvar and went into the mountains, where, it is said, he dwelt among the vilas.

Yet I have also heard others say that he remained a dragon unto the end of his days, and in time died of old age. His bones, they claim, lie beneath the mound beyond the tower.

Within the great library there remain unto this day enormous bones which some believe belonged unto Vesurrod.

Yet they may as well have belonged unto a agras, for many such creatures dwelt in those lands in ancient times.

Hant died and was buried with the others.

After him came the second Narko, who took that name in honour of the former master.

It was he who planted the cloister garden that grows there unto this day.

## IX. The Tale of Berglar’s Vengeance and the End of the Text
The brother of Vesurrod did not forgive him.

He was among those who urged the king to slay the dragon when it was brought unto Moldvar.

Yet he also obeyed the king’s decree.

When the appointed years had passed and that king had died, Berglar—for such was his name—gathered men unto himself and set forth against the brethren.

And with him went Kimen, who later joined the brethren and took part in the founding of the library at Vosdena.

While on the road they learned that Vesurrod was gone, whether dead or departed.

Yet Berglar did not turn back.

He halted at the village of Thunglauks[^10] and there took counsel concerning what should be done.

And it was resolved that Moldvar must be burned, together with those who dwelt therein.

Yet not all among Berglar’s company were pleased by this decision.

Then Berglar gathered his men, and by night they approached the manor, where....

*Here the manuscript breaks off.*

## Notes
[1] Thomgravs — The Oakwood. The Norician name derives from the Areile Tōmgrava, literally “Oak Slope” or “Oak Hillside.”  

[2] Ruganskaps — Literally “Tomb of the Vilas.” The location is otherwise unknown. It may have referred to the burial place of King Kalvedil.  

[3] The Insuveses of Andomir — Most likely an anachronism, for at the time of the founding of the hermitage—and thus in the earliest years of Lingvar—there were as yet no wars with Andomir.  

[4] Agint — A name otherwise unattested and inconsistent with the noble naming customs of Lingvar.  

[5] Vesurrod — The name literally means “Born of a Lizard.”  

[6] The Strait — Refers to the Strait of Ansalgis, which separates the island of Lingvar from Norycia.  

[7] Andsar — A popular Norician festival marking the beginning of spring.  

[8] Lethbrost — Literally “Ice Bridge.” During the flight of the vilas to the south, the Nasel Sea was said to have frozen over, allowing them to pass into Noricia.  

[9] The Song of the Names of Trees — Refers to Ainarai Enoiani, a song of the vilas enumerating the most renowned trees.  

[10] Thunglauks — A fictitious village name. Literally “Field of Wolves.” It was likely intended to emphasize Berglar’s murderous intent.
